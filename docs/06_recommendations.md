# Recommendations

What I would do differently, ranked by estimated economic impact where the analysis supports a number. Figures marked *(estimated)* are sized from the analysis; figures marked *(judgement)* are operator recommendations that the data supports directionally but does not quantify directly.

---

## 1. Raise batch sizes on the highest-volume items

**Source:** F1 · **Impact:** ~₹19,500/month per outlet *(estimated)*

The analysis showed that limited-batch production was saving only about ₹2/day of waste while exposing the business to roughly ₹642/day of foregone contribution on days when 1–2 items stocked out. The visible cost of waste was being optimised at the expense of a much larger invisible cost: lost sales.

**How:** Increase batch sizes selectively for the top five items by volume, especially on expected high-demand days. Measure the resulting waste increase against the contribution recovered from fewer stockouts.

**Risk:** On days already above roughly 80% kitchen utilisation, larger batches consume constrained preparation capacity. Apply the change first on normal-demand days and treat peak-day batching as a separate capacity decision.

---

## 2. Test breakeven before opening another outlet

**Source:** B2, D3 · **Impact:** up to ~₹4.77 L of Outlet 3 lifetime loss exposure *(estimated; not necessarily fully avoidable)*

The expansion analysis showed that the newer outlets added revenue but were net-negative over their operating periods. Thoraipakkam generated ₹18.30 L of gross revenue but lost approximately ₹4.77 L of outlet net across 21 months.

**How:** Before signing a new lease, calculate the expected contribution per order and the order volume required to cover the new outlet's fixed costs. Set a defined ramp-up period and a stop-loss threshold before committing further capital.

**Risk:** Outlet performance cannot be separated completely from launch timing or external shocks. Thoraipakkam opened during COVID, so its observed loss should not be treated as proof that the location itself would always have failed.

---

## 3. Manage weekdays and weekends as different operating models

**Source:** D4 · **Impact:** *(judgement)* — the analysis quantifies the economic difference but does not establish a recoverable annual ₹ amount

Weekdays averaged 24 orders and produced only ₹193 of surplus after daily fixed cost. Weekends averaged 53.6 orders and produced ₹4,563. The business therefore depended on two days of the week for almost all of its profit.

**How:** Keep weekday operations lean and focus on covering fixed cost. Protect weekend availability, preparation capacity and throughput because a lost order on a weekend carries far more economic weight.

**Risk:** Weekend concentration can make the business vulnerable to weather, platform disruption or other shocks affecting peak days. The objective is not to ignore weekdays, but to allocate resources according to their economic value.

---

## 4. Separate restaurant-funded and platform-funded discounts in every financial analysis

**Source:** B4 · **Impact:** prevents a ~₹40,100/month promotional-cost misstatement *(estimated accounting impact)*

Customers saw an average discount of 10.93% of gross, but only 2.50% was restaurant-funded. Platform-funded discounts accounted for 77.2% of discount depth.

**How:** Keep the two funding streams as separate fields in the transactional model and in management reporting. Evaluate promotional performance using the restaurant-funded cost and resulting contribution rather than the headline customer-visible discount.

**Risk:** Correct funding attribution does not prove that a promotion created incremental profitable demand. A holdout or comparable control would be required to measure incrementality.

---

## 5. Use outlet-specific breakeven thresholds before spending more on growth

**Source:** D3 · **Impact:** *(judgement)* — the threshold is quantified; the avoided-loss value depends on the subsequent management decision

The reconstructed economics put Kandanchavadi's breakeven at about 17.5 orders/day on outlet costs alone and 22.6 orders/day when company overhead is loaded. My recalled operating floor of roughly 20 orders/day falls between those two figures.

**How:** Use approximately 15 orders/day as a warning threshold for a lower-cost outlet, around 18 orders/day for a higher-cost outlet, and around 23 orders/day when evaluating whether the outlet is covering its share of company overhead. Trigger a cost review before increasing acquisition spend.

**Risk:** The thresholds depend on contribution per order and fixed-cost scope. They should be recalculated if either changes materially.

---

## 6. Protect the 55-minute delivery threshold

**Source:** F2 · **Impact:** *(judgement)* — service impact is measurable; rupee impact is not

Average ratings stayed around 4.38 up to 55 minutes, then fell to 3.45. The share of 1–2 star ratings increased from 6.1% to 26.3%, a 4.3× increase.

**How:** Use 45 minutes as the normal target, 45–55 minutes as a monitoring range, and >55 minutes as an intervention trigger. Investigate kitchen and rider bottlenecks before the order crosses the threshold.

**Risk:** Delivery time is not the only driver of ratings. Food temperature, order accuracy and packaging condition may also contribute and cannot be isolated in this dataset.

---

## 7. Concentrate the menu around proven demand and economics

**Source:** C2 · **Impact:** *(judgement)* — no guaranteed savings can be assigned from the classification alone

The retrospective Kasavana–Smith analysis identified six Stars, three Plowhorses, three Puzzles and five Dogs among the 17 main-menu items.

**How:** Protect the six Stars. Review the five Dogs first for possible removal, beginning with the weakest items. Attempt to improve the three Plowhorses through pricing, recipe or cost changes. Try positioning or bundling the three Puzzles before considering delisting them.

**Risk:** Four of the five Dogs are vegetarian, and the vegetarian range was added in response to customer demand. Removing items purely on margin and volume could reduce category coverage or substitution opportunities.

**Important limitation:** This is a retrospective analysis of reconstructed data. It does not establish the exact historical month in which any item should have been delisted.

---

## 8. Manage peak capacity before investing in a bigger kitchen

**Source:** F3 · **Impact:** *(judgement)* — preparation-time impact is measurable; kitchen-expansion ROI is not

Preparation time remained around 20–21 minutes at normal utilisation but increased to 29.2 minutes at 132% utilisation. The data therefore shows a capacity constraint at high utilisation, particularly during peaks.

**How:** Keep normal utilisation around 70–80%, monitor preparation time as utilisation rises, and first test staffing, batch scheduling and peak-hour order controls. Consider additional physical capacity only if demand consistently exceeds the available capacity after those interventions.

**Risk:** Larger batches recommended by F1 also consume kitchen capacity. Inventory and capacity decisions therefore need to be coordinated rather than optimised independently.

---

## 9. Target larger baskets rather than relying on mean AOV

**Source:** E3 · **Impact:** ~₹3.6 L incremental contribution across the analysed period under the stated 10% migration scenario *(estimated illustrative sizing, not a forecast)*

The ₹250–349 band represented 45.7% of orders but only 33.0% of revenue, while the ₹600–799 band represented 18.9% of orders and 29.2% of revenue. The mean AOV of ₹427 described very few actual orders.

**How:** Report median AOV and basket-band mix alongside the mean. Target the ₹250–349 band with two-main combos, minimum-order thresholds and selected add-on offers. Use high-margin Puzzle items as potential second-slot items where appropriate.

**Risk:** The estimated contribution assumes customers actually move between basket bands and does not measure whether an incentive is required to cause the change. The estimate is therefore an opportunity-sizing exercise, not a forecast.

---

## 10. Treat platform concentration as a standing business risk

**Source:** B5 · **Impact:** *(judgement)*

The UberEats exit changed the platform mix without producing an immediate disappearance of demand; Swiggy and Zomato continued carrying the business. However, the business remained dependent on external platforms and had no direct customer channel.

**How:** Track platform share, orders and contribution per order weekly after any major platform change. Maintain a contingency plan for platform exits, commercial-term changes or reduced promotional support.

**Risk:** The dataset can demonstrate aggregate platform substitution but cannot prove that individual UberEats customers migrated to Swiggy or Zomato because customer identity is platform-scoped.

# What I would not do

### Build a bigger kitchen immediately

**Why:** F3 shows the constraint primarily at high utilisation. Scheduling, staffing, batch planning and peak-hour controls should be tested before committing additional fixed cost.

### Delist all five Dog items immediately

**Why:** C2 identifies them as the weakest items on the two selected dimensions, but four are vegetarian and category coverage has strategic value. The classification is a review trigger, not an automatic deletion rule.

### Chase weekday volume indiscriminately

**Why:** Weekdays generated only 9.6% of profit despite representing 71.5% of the calendar. Additional weekday volume is not automatically valuable if it requires materially higher acquisition or operating cost.

### Treat platform-funded discounts as free growth

**Why:** B4 establishes who paid for the discount, not whether the promotion created incremental profitable demand. The business was also exposed to the platform's promotional decisions.

### Use cumulative accounting profit as a proxy for cash reserves

**Why:** D5 measures accumulated company net, not a bank balance. A future business should explicitly manage liquidity rather than assume accounting profit is available as cash.

---

# Priority Order

The recommendations fall into three decision groups:

**Immediate operating changes:**  
Batch sizing, weekday/weekend operating discipline, delivery-time control.

**Capital-allocation controls:**  
Outlet-level breakeven testing and peak-capacity management.

**Measurement and commercial discipline:**  
Discount attribution, menu review, basket management and platform-concentration monitoring.

The common principle is the same across all ten recommendations:

> **Optimise for contribution and survivability, not for the easiest cost or metric to see.**
